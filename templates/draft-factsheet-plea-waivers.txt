HARVEY AND CRUZ WAIVERS: WHAT YOU ARE GIVING UP
The Citizen Movement -- thecitizenmovement.org

At a plea hearing the judge may ask you to "waive Harvey" or "waive
Cruz." These are real rights you are being asked to surrender. Know
what they mean before you say yes.

WHAT IS A HARVEY WAIVER

- From People v. Harvey. It comes up when you plead guilty to some
  counts and the prosecution dismisses the rest.
- Normally, the judge cannot consider dismissed counts when deciding
  your sentence.
- A Harvey waiver gives up that protection: it lets the judge look at
  the facts behind the dismissed counts when sentencing you --
  including when ordering restitution to victims.
- Example: you plead to one theft count and two are dismissed. With a
  Harvey waiver, the judge can still consider all three thefts at
  sentencing.

WHAT IS A CRUZ WAIVER

- From People v. Cruz. It comes up when you plead guilty but sentencing
  happens on a later date.
- A Cruz waiver means: if you fail to appear for sentencing, or you
  commit a new crime before sentencing, you lose the right to withdraw
  your plea -- and the judge can sentence you more harshly than the
  deal promised.
- Without the waiver, missing your court date would generally let you
  take back the plea. With it, you are stuck with whatever sentence
  the judge imposes.

BEFORE YOU SAY YES -- ASK YOUR LAWYER

- Which counts are being dismissed, and what facts could the judge
  consider under a Harvey waiver?
- What sentence range am I facing with the waiver versus without it?
- Under a Cruz waiver, exactly what happens if I miss my court date
  or get arrested on something new?
- Is there any part of this deal I can negotiate instead of waiving?

YOU CAN SAY NO

- You are allowed to refuse either waiver. But understand the trade:
  the prosecution may withdraw the plea offer and take the case to
  trial on all counts.
- Never agree to a waiver you do not understand. Ask the judge to
  explain it on the record in plain language before you answer.
