EXPUNGEMENT IN CALIFORNIA -- STEP-BY-STEP GUIDE
Penal Code section 1203.4

This is a guide, not a court form. The actual forms are Judicial Council
forms CR-180 (Petition for Dismissal) and CR-181 (Order for Dismissal),
available free at courts.ca.gov/forms.

MY CASE INFORMATION -- FILL THIS IN FIRST

Full legal name: [YOUR FULL NAME]
Case number: [CASE NUMBER]
County of conviction: [COUNTY]
Date of conviction: [DATE]
Charge(s): [CHARGE AND CODE SECTION -- EXAMPLE: "PC 484 Petty Theft"]
Probation end date: [DATE PROBATION ENDED, OR "STILL ON PROBATION"]

WHAT EXPUNGEMENT DOES

Under Penal Code section 1203.4, an expungement (legally called a
"dismissal") reopens your case, withdraws your guilty or no-contest
plea, and dismisses the charge. After that, for most private purposes
-- job applications, housing, background checks by private employers --
you may lawfully answer that you were not convicted of that offense.

WHAT EXPUNGEMENT DOES NOT DO -- READ THIS HONESTLY

An expungement is not an eraser. Know the limits before you file:

- You must still disclose the conviction when applying for government
  employment, a professional license, public office, or a job as a
  peace officer.
- The dismissed case can still be used against you as a prior
  conviction in a future criminal case.
- It does not restore firearm rights lost due to a felony conviction.
- Federal agencies and immigration authorities can still see it.
- It does not seal or destroy the record; the case file still exists.

STEP 1 -- CHECK YOUR ELIGIBILITY

You are generally eligible if ALL of the following are true:

[ ] You were convicted of a misdemeanor or a felony for which you were
    granted probation (not sent to state prison, with limited exceptions
    for certain realignment cases).
[ ] You have successfully completed probation, or probation was
    terminated early.
[ ] You are not currently on probation for any offense.
[ ] You are not currently charged with, or serving a sentence for, any
    new offense.
[ ] You have no new criminal cases pending.

If you were denied probation or went to prison, different rules apply
-- ask the court clerk or a legal aid office about Penal Code sections
1203.4a and 1203.41.

STEP 2 -- GET THE FORMS

Download free from courts.ca.gov/forms:
- CR-180: Petition for Dismissal
- CR-181: Order for Dismissal (the judge signs this one)

Fill in your name, case number, and the conviction details. The case
number is on your original court paperwork; the clerk can look it up
if you lost it.

STEP 3 -- FILE WITH THE COURT

File the CR-180 (plus 2 copies) with the clerk of the superior court
in the county where you were convicted -- NOT the county where you
live now, unless they are the same.

Filing fee: varies by county (often $60-$150). If you cannot afford
it, file a fee waiver request (Form FW-001) at the same time.

Give a copy to the district attorney's office in that county -- the
clerk can tell you whether the court serves it or you must.

STEP 4 -- THE HEARING

Many courts grant straightforward petitions without a hearing. If a
hearing is set, dress respectfully, arrive early, and be ready to tell
the judge briefly why you deserve relief: employment, schooling,
housing, family stability. Bring proof of rehabilitation if you have
it (pay stubs, school records, program completions).

STEP 5 -- AFTER THE ORDER

Once the judge signs the CR-181 order, get certified copies from the
clerk (small fee per copy). Send a copy to the Department of Justice
so your state criminal history is updated. Keep a certified copy with
your important papers forever.

TIMELINE: From filing to order is typically 1-3 months depending on
the county. Start early if you need it for a job or license.

COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

- Filing in the wrong county. It must be the county of conviction.
- Filing while still on probation. Finish probation first, or ask the
  court to terminate probation early.
- Forgetting the district attorney needs notice.
- Assuming it seals the record. It does not -- it dismisses the
  conviction, which is still powerful, but different.

This guide provides general legal information only -- not legal
advice. Consult a licensed attorney for your situation.
